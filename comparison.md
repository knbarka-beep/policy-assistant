I tested the three methods on the same 26 questions (Table 1 and Table 2). Sixteen can be answered directly from a policy, four touch a policy that lacks the detail asked for, such as a maximum gift value, and six are not covered by the database at all. Rules-based search found the right policy for 19 of 20 answerable questions at no cost and in no measurable time, but it has no sense of when to stop. It answered three of the six uncovered questions with an unrelated policy, for example returning the probation policy for a question about resignation notice, and it only returns the policy text, so the employee still has to work out the answer. Both LLM methods found the right policy for all 20, declined all six uncovered questions and did not invent a single number. On the four questions with missing detail they said the policy does not specify it instead of guessing, which was the main hallucination risk the test was built around. An answer counts as unsupported if it cites a policy that is not in the database, or states a number that appears neither in the cited policy nor in the question.

I prefer the LLM with a vector index. It matched the LLM without an index on accuracy while using about 295 tokens per question against 2.267, roughly 87 % fewer, because it sends the three closest policies instead of all 98. With only 98 short policies the whole file still fits easily in one prompt, so today the index mainly saves cost. Its real advantage is that it keeps working when the policy base grows beyond what a prompt can hold. The response times, a median of about 8 s against 10 s, say more about Gemini's free tier than about the methods, since around 11 of 26 calls per method fell back from an overloaded model to the next one. The index also adds a risk of its own: if the right policy is not among the three it retrieves, the model never sees it. That did not happen here, but it is the first thing I would monitor. Rules-based search is still useful as a zero-cost fallback when the API is down.
